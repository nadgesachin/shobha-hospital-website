Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS, also called PCOD) is one of the most common hormonal conditions in women of reproductive age. It affects periods, skin, weight and fertility — but with the right care, most women with PCOS manage it well and go on to have healthy pregnancies.

## What is PCOS?

PCOS is a hormonal imbalance in which the ovaries do not release an egg regularly. Many women with PCOS also have higher levels of androgens (sometimes called "male hormones") and a body that responds less efficiently to insulin. The name comes from the many small follicles that can be seen on the ovaries in an ultrasound, but you do not need to have "cysts" to have PCOS.

## Common signs and symptoms

Symptoms vary a lot from woman to woman. The most common are:

- Irregular, delayed or missed periods
- Acne, especially along the jaw and chin
- Extra hair growth on the face, chest or abdomen
- Thinning hair on the scalp
- Weight gain, particularly around the waist, or difficulty losing weight
- Dark, velvety patches of skin on the neck or underarms
- Difficulty getting pregnant

## Why does PCOS happen?

The exact cause is not fully understood. It usually comes from a combination of:

- **Genetics** — PCOS often runs in families.
- **Insulin resistance** — the body needs more insulin to keep blood sugar normal, and high insulin can push the ovaries to make more androgens.
- **Lifestyle factors** — low physical activity, irregular sleep, stress and a diet high in refined carbohydrates can make symptoms worse.

## How is PCOS diagnosed?

Your gynecologist will take a detailed history of your periods and symptoms, examine you, and usually recommend:

- Blood tests for hormones, thyroid, blood sugar and cholesterol
- A pelvic ultrasound to look at the ovaries and the lining of the uterus

PCOS is generally diagnosed when at least two of these are present: irregular ovulation, signs of high androgens, and polycystic ovaries on ultrasound — after other causes have been ruled out.

## How PCOS is managed

There is no single "cure", but symptoms can be controlled very effectively. Treatment is personalised to your goals — regular periods, clearer skin, weight management or pregnancy.

### Lifestyle comes first

- **Movement:** about 150 minutes of moderate activity a week (brisk walking, cycling, yoga) improves insulin sensitivity.
- **Food:** prefer whole grains, dals, vegetables, fruits, nuts and protein at every meal; cut down on sugar, maida, fried snacks and sweetened drinks.
- **Weight:** even a 5–10% reduction in weight can bring back regular cycles in many women.
- **Sleep and stress:** regular sleep timings and stress management (pranayama, meditation) support hormonal balance.

### Medical treatment

Depending on your needs, your doctor may suggest medicines to regulate periods, reduce androgen symptoms, improve insulin response, or help ovulation if you are trying to conceive. These should only be taken under medical supervision.

## When should you see a doctor?

Book a consultation if you have:

- Fewer than 8 periods a year, or gaps of more than 35 days between periods
- No period for 3 months (and you are not pregnant)
- Very heavy or prolonged bleeding
- Sudden increase in facial or body hair
- Been trying to conceive for 12 months (or 6 months if you are over 35)

> PCOS is a long-term condition, but it is very manageable. Early evaluation also lowers the long-term risk of diabetes, high cholesterol and endometrial problems.
